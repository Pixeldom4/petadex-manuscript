\section{Introduction}

By the end of 2026, an estimated 13 billion tonnes, or 13 Gigatonnes (GT), of plastic waste will have been produced globally \todo{plastic pollution citation here} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro}A,C). About half of the waste will have been managed through recycling or incineration, but the fate of the rest remains unaccounted for in the environment. Plastic-degrading enzymes represent a promising solution to this plastic problem. These enzymes are capable of degrading plastics down into its constituent monomers, which are then available for the repolymerization of new plastic-products, or biodegradation (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro}B--D).

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{petadex-manuscript-figure.1_plastic-degrading-enzyme-introduction.png}
\caption{\textbf{Plastic waste and its enzymatic degradation.} \textbf{(A)} Cumulative global plastic waste by fate, 1950--2050. \textbf{(B)} IsPETase hydrolyses PET to the monomer BHET, then to TPA and EG, feeding biomass or recycling. \textbf{(C)} Carbon mass by category; plastics (6.7~Gt) exceed all animals (2.5~Gt) 2.6-fold. \textbf{(D)} IsPETase active site: Ser160--His237--Asp206 triad with bound PET dimer. \textbf{(E)} Logan assembles 27~million SRA experiments into contigs.}
\label{fig:intro}
\end{figure*}

Existing databases of plastic-degrading enzymes are limited in scope and biodiversity. A notable database, the plastics-active enzymes database (PAZy), contains 495 functionally verified plastic-active enzymes. This dataset is constrained by limited diversity, with most enzymes being homologs of LCC or IsPETase. Sequence-based mining for the discovery of new enzymes is a well established method, with Danso \textit{et al.}, 2018 \todo{cite} having discovered 504 candidates from database searches on UniprotKB and NCBI-nr, alongside 349 candidates from mining 133 metagenomes. More recent attempts, such as Erickson \textit{et al.}, 2022 \todo{cite} expanded by screening 87 targeted hot-spring metagenomes ($\sim$38 million sequences) for plastic-degrading enzymes, characterizing 74 putative enzymes, and further testing 51 for activity, and characterizing 23 novel plastic-active enzymes. Overall, these previous surveys have merely scrapped the surface of the plastic-degrading enzyme diversity that exists within metagenomic datasets.

In our previous work, we build Logan \todo{cite}, a tool that assembled the entire SRA, 27.3 million metagenomic datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro}E), and searched for plastic-degrading enzymes, retrieving 215.7M putative plastic-degrading enzymes from an input query of 213 characterized plastic-degrading enzymes. We demonstrated that the discovery of novel plastic-degrading enzymes within this dataset is possible through operationally random selection. However, the lack of annotations prevents strategic selection of putative plastic-degrading enzymes for validation.

Here we present PETadex, methodological and quantitative rebuild of our previous dataset. This novel dataset supports the targeted selection of enzymes with plastic-degrading activity. Using an input dataset of 411 plastic-degrading enzymes, we searched NCBI-nr and the SRA (using the Logan assembly), constraining results using catalytic-residue-constrained profile HMMs. As a result, we've built a dataset of \todo{x} million enzymes that carry the catalytic domain boundaries, annotated with fold-assignments, protein-structures and the temperature/pH of the biosamples of origin. This enables candidate plastic-degrading enzymes to be filtered based on catalytic integrity, structural fold, and metadata properties prior to testing. This enables the high throughput targeted-selection of candidates, a process which can be further automated using innovative machine learning methods. The dataset of enzymes, annotations and a toolkit of applicable methods are freely available at \url{https://petadex.net}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sequence and structure-based annotation of 609 known plastic-degrading enzymes}

Following established workflows for the homology-based retrieval and expansion of a given 
set of enzymes of interest \todo{cite Erickson, Danso, Logan etc.}, we built a workflow for 
the retrieval of plastic-degrading enzymes from NCBI-nr and the SRA (using the Logan assembly).

Improving upon our previous PETadex dataset, by updating the input search query and fixing 
several methodological limitations, we present the updated PETadex v2 dataset.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{petadex-manuscript-figure.2_petadex-dataset.png}
\caption{\textbf{The PETadex-260701 dataset.} \textbf{(A)} Composition of the plasticome: the 609-enzyme union of the two PAZy snapshots, its collapse to 411 cluster centroids at 90\% amino acid identity, the 46 graph components those centroids resolve into, and the CATH folds assigned to them, coloured by provenance. \textbf{(B)} Overview of PETadex generation, from the 411 input sequences through the PETadex-nr and PETadex-logan expansions. \textbf{(C)} Hierarchical clustering of catalytic sequences into enzymes (90\% identity), families (60\%), superfamilies (30\%) and distinct folds. \textbf{(D)} Cluster size distributions at the protein, enzyme and family levels.}
\label{fig:dataset}
\end{figure*}

To generate this updated PETadex-260701 dataset, we created a starting search query from 
combining two versions of the PAZy database, the first from the original PETadex generation 
(pazy-240826; $n=212$; August 26th, 2024), and a recently updated version (pazy-260701; 
$n=473$; July 1st, 2026). Sourcing 136 enzymes from pazy-240826, 397 enzymes from pazy-260701 
and 76 merged between both, generating a 609 enzyme union set (plasticome). Plasticome was 
then clustered at 90\% amino acid identity to 411 cluster centroids (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}A). Predicting structures of 
these centroids using ESMFold2 (ESMC-6B trunk), then searches against the CATH S40 database 
with Foldseek alongside searching against InterProScan resolved the proteins into 15 distinct 
protein domains. These centroids are further aligned into 46 graph components ($\ge$30\% amino 
acid identity and $E < 1\times10^{-5}$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}A).

\subsection{Homology-based retrieval yields a dataset of 215.7 million putative plastic-degrading enzymes}

To expand the dataset of known plastic-degrading enzymes beyond the plasticome set, we first searched these 
411 centroids into the NCBI nr database using DIAMOND(DIAMOND v2.2.4.184, \texttt{-{}-very-sensitive}, 
\texttt{-k0}), recovering 42,053,081 pairwise alignments, collapsing to 8,850,583 unique sequences \todo{cite}. Of these,
7,825,535 pass the e-value filter ($E \le 1\times10^{-5}$), and further removing plasticome self-hits returns
a final count of 7,825,107 sequences (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}B).

In order to search Logan with only the catalytic domains of these plasticome homologs, we built profile HMMs to
characterize the plastic-degrading domain boundaries and catalytic residues. These HMMs were built from seed MSAs
(MUSCLE 5.3) of plasticome sequences together with their closest nr homologs ($E \le 1\times10^{-50}$), where each member of
the alignments must contain the residues involved in the catalytic mechanism, annotated from literature \todo{cite}. 
The 46 graph components were further segmented using these catalytic mechanisms, resolving into 53 MSAs.
The HMMs were built from the seed MSAs using a pipeline analogous to JACKhmmer \todo{cite}. HMMs were created using
HMMbuild, and then searched (HMMsearch) against close NCBI-nr homologs ($E \le 1\times10^{-10}$), annotating the catalytic
residues \todo{cite}. Alignments were created from sequences that were below the e-value cut-off of 1.0E-10 and 
contained all of the canonical catalytic residues. These MSAs serve as the seed MSAs for the next iteration of the pipeline.
While creating the next iteration's HMMs, match and insert state annotations were progated from the previous HMM and
any column of the alignment that had an occupancy below 0.7 was declared an insert state. The pipeline was then
repeated for a total of 4 iterations.

Searching these component HMMs against the 7,825,107 nr sequences yielded 7,562,397 HMM hits. Of these hits,
the HMM range of 6,857,582 contained all of the positions of the catalytic residues ("complete hits"),
of which 6,343,582 contained the canonical catalytic residue identities. The ranges annotated as the catalytic
domains were then clustered (DIAMOND --linclust), resulting in 2,740,458 90\% centroids (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}B). These centroids serve
as the input query into Logan.

Expanding on the nr search, we queried these \todo{x} million enzyme homologs across Logan (version \todo{x})
contigs, recovering \todo{x} billion matching sequences (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}B). Of these Logan sequences, \todo{x} matched an nr
enzyme at 90\% identity, and clustering the remaining \todo{x} at 90\% aaid, yielding \todo{x} million
non-redundant enzyme homologs (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}C,D). We've dubbed this dataset PETadex-v2, with sub sets dubbed PETadex-plasticome
(or simply plasticome), PETadex-nr and PETadex-logan, depending on the origin. \todo{Describe a domain from
the search, can be a/B hydrolase, Amydases, etc.}

\subsection{Structural, environmental and taxonomic annotations of the PETadex dataset}

To explore and utilize the PETadex dataset, we annotated the sequences along four axes. Using metadata, structural, environmental and 
taxonomic information. We first annotated these sequences with structures, folding each of the superfamily (/30\% centroids) the structures using ESMFold2 (ESMC-6B trunk).
We then annotated the sequences with domains, the catalytic domain boundaries and the catalytic residues, alongside accessory domain annotations that may 
additionally contribute to the catalytic mechanism. Furthermore, we enriched the dataset using environmental and taxonomic metadata such as geographical locations (Fig.~\ref{fig:metadata}B),
the organisms that the sequences came from (Fig.~\ref{fig:metadata}A), and the environmental conditions they were collected from.

These annotations were then applied towards the dataset to enable homology-based characterization of previously uncharacterized enzymes following metadata filtering. 
Furthermore, these metadata features are useful for the discovery and selection of novel enzymes of interest for future characterization experiments.
